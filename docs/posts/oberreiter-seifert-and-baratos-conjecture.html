Oberreiter, Seifert and Barato’s Conjecture on Entropy Production by Biochemical Clocks

24 August 2026 · And Some Other Thoughts

I am reading a paper by Oberreiter, Seifert and Barato [1]. I found it from GPT Sol whilst trying to do more mathematical conjecture-hunting on Sol Ultra. I wanted to see if there was any progress that I could make in the area of non-equilibrium statistical mechanics, something that I've been working on, along with its links to biology.

My main method of attack given these new developments in AI has to try and see if there are any conjectures that can be disproven or proven through elementary methods that I am actually able to understand. And then I would go about then parsing the generated results by hand, or with tools like the ones my friend George Smith has made. I am hoping for both results and something of pedagogical value to me. Such records of parsing seem to be what other people are doing, like Terence Tao's recent 'digestions' of AI generated results.

I was slightly emboldened by my progress that I made on a conjecture in random matrices last week (I possibly resolved a conjecture). On that project, I emailed the solution to the authors who then replied saying that the solution looks correct. (More on this later)

The downside of this approach of constructing understandable counterexamples is that my current level and knowledge of math is the limiting factor, and so proofs that I force AIs to generate with methods known to me are likely to be long winded and probably don't have some big insight. Also, it seems less aesthetic than trying to come up with a motivated, constructive theory to resolve something, which is something that I've struggled to get AI to do. If I wanted to solve a problem with AI assistance through theory building, which is how most of current research mathematics seems to be done, then the use of AI doesn't really seem appropriate for someone with my aims. I suspect that I would get lost instantly due to complexity. Also, counterexamples only resolve a conjecture when it's false - there are many conjectures which are likely true and it's unclear how an independent mathematician could contribute a convincing result of something outside of the counter-example type stuff that we see AI doing.

As a companion side project, I am also trying to parse the book on non-equilibrium process by De Groot and Mazur, specifically on chemical reactions. On the experimental side I am still trying to find more non-linear phenomena that you can observe safely at home, like the Briggs Rauscher reaction or Liesegang rings. I failed in my last attempt at Briggs Rauscher which I will write up soon.

Anyways, back to my rough initial thoughts on the paper.

Consider the system of processes that drive our circadian rhythms. They oscillate with a noisy period of twenty-four hours. There seem to be many such biological processes that oscillate (albeit with noise). A biochemical clock is a system in which the concentrations of the chemicals involved oscillate at a near-constant period. Such clocks vary in mechanism. Some involve gene regulation; others require no gene regulation at all and can be assembled purely from biochemicals in a test tube. The paper cites one such protein system [2].

We expect noise in biological environments to affect biochemical oscillators, so it seems reasonable that the oscillations decay in time. The paper uses a measure called the number of coherent oscillations [3] to quantify how long they last. I won't go into too much detail on exactly what this means in this post - the reader can check the references.

The main result of Oberreiter, Seifert and Barato is a conjecture that bounds from below the entropy produced by a biochemical oscillator, where the bound is a function of the number of coherent oscillations. Bounds of these form our cool because it might be able to let us constrain the energetics of some biological processes - an approach that Nick Lane talks about in his book The Vital Question [4]. In the early chapters he also makes a nod to the dissipative structures work by Prigogine.

To the best of my knowledge, this conjecture remains unproven. Right now I am attempting to get GPT Sol to solve it - it has made a counter example and I am trying to check it rigorously. I am emboldened somewhat by my last submission of a counterexample in random matrix theory, which the original authors said "looks correct" in an email eight days after I sent it to them. (I am not really sure what to do with it, though; it feels like publishing such a thing through formal channels would be a waste of time.)

The preliminary counterexample that Sol came up with is here. GPT Sol first reframes the problem in terms of the eigenvalues of the system and then tries to massage a network to fulfil the inequality without changing the entropy.

This counterexample looks correct at first glance. I'm still trying to digest it by going through the proof manually. On the surface it looks like Sol constructs a bilayer network, one of which is in equilibrium, and one which isn't, such that the slow eigenvalues can be massaged without moving the entropy that much.

Right now, if this example was correct, it would indeed be pretty contrived. It would be cool to find other such counterexamples with the aim of finding a biochemical clock that is below this bound.

In other news, I am finding learning statistical mechanics this way to be a lot more fun than trying to parse textbooks. I have four textbooks right now but I've been struggling to find a mental 'hook' to get this stuff into my brain! We might be able to motivate younger students more by trying to mass-find low hanging conjectures for them to chew on, or to find higher quality digestions of.

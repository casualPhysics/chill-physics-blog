Minimal Assumptions for Societal Resilience

25 September 2026 · My old biosecurity work, diverse testing, and the uncertainties moving forward.

Last year I was deep into doing biosecurity and pandemic prevention experiments at home. One project involved testing DIY air purifiers with air quality monitors and matches. Another project was a baseline investigation into the effect of UV on yeast. By design, my experiments were designed to be replicable at home, using both cheap materials and instrumentation. I did this because I thought that making experiments DIY would be a way for everyone to trust the science, to be able to do it themselves, hopefully learn something about biosecurity, and maybe learn something about empiricism in a world now fraught with deep uncertainty.

Side note, I'm more excited about home experimentation being a model of change to a more well calibrated public - doing experiments at home certainly helped me with my understanding of what empiricism is. This is what we try to do at the Independent Science Society.*

I'm happy to see other people reading my previous work on biosecurity projects, which was featured in BlueDot Impact's biosecurity curriculum. I still think there are a bunch of low-hanging fruit in terms of biosafety experiments done by regular people at home. For example, how could we visualise aerosol movements in a set of diverse environments? Is there a way to measure the impact of a sneeze? There are also non-technical experiments that I think are cool too, to do with getting lots of data. For example, how fast might it be for the average person in London to get access to an elastomeric respirator?

And by design, it's probably a good thing that the experiments can be done at home or by the general public. This is because having data on how someone might test biosafety in a low resource environment is still valuable no matter how the experiment goes. For example, if someone is trying to make a Corsi-Rosenthal box, but doesn't see the concentration of particulates falling, then it could mean two things.

First, a failure to make the Corsi-Rosenthal box properly. Someone who is willing to take the time out of their day to test biosecurity is probably competent. And so a failure to see success here would be valuable data - we shouldn't make the person feel bad for not being able to do it, and incentives shouldn't be tied towards a successful experiment. If the CR box fails in the hands of a reasonable person, then the construction is not simple enough, and we need to revisit. If a sufficiently motivated person isn't able to do it, then we shouldn't expect the general public to be able to, which is the whole point.

Or, in the case that the CR box does work, but the experiment still fails, then it could be faulty sensors. In this case, it still would be of value to write down exactly what brand of sensors failed and why they failed, and a full description of how the experiment went. This type of stuff is useful; when I go to Alibaba to buy cheap sensors, some of which may prove to be cheap and effective tests, it's very hard to know what works or not. I found what I thought was a Far UVC lamp from China, which I don't think it is anymore. In a neglected area like biosecurity, honest and high fidelity data is valuable. It gives us information about things in a wide range of settings.

But anyways, for the ten people who have been following my blog from the beginning, you may realise that I kind of semi-stopped working on biosecurity. I still think about it a lot but did fewer active experiments, research, and writing on the topic.

There are a few reasons for this. I am a bit of a nerd, and one reason I took my foot off the gas was aesthetic interest. I found the physical and technical biodefence stuff quite interesting, but not, say, as interesting as cutting edge physics of life stuff that I've been looking at recently. And I also thought that a lot of the theory of change in biosecurity probably lay in policy, and I wasn't sure about how to feel about working on something adjacent to the main thing. When I work on something, I prefer to be at the tip of the spear.

Also, I was confused about what to do. And I wasn't really sure about how to be useful.

Why was I confused about what to do? Frankly put, I had a large amount of uncertainty in both the magnitude and qualitative nature of biosecurity risks. At the time, it was unclear how large the risk was, and I was worried that I was working too much on something with a very very very minuscule risk (i.e. the level of me winning the lottery). To be clear, it's not that I thought the risk was very very very minuscule, it's just that I was uncomfortable with the amount of uncertainty I had in assessing that risk. My estimates were from very very very minuscule to small, and that range was super blurry. And the state space of actions at that time still seemed really dependent on where my future better estimates were in that range. This was also a time when the capabilities of LLMs were still pretty unclear.

On the usefulness front, I also had epistemic uncertainty about what other people outside my sphere were doing, and was wondering if I was just doubling effort. Who knows.

I don't believe that the risk is very very very minuscule, but if it were, I think it would've been justified to not work on it. It's probably ok not to work on tail risks that are very very small, even if they could be bad. For example, at this point in time, I don't think I could justify working on mitigating the risks of a meteor strike on the earth. Even if a meteor strike is certainly an existential event! There's also an amount of cookage that's involved in the calculus. If there is a meteor coming, it's likely that we're cooked, and so there's no point in working, and we should enjoy our lives before the meteor burns us all.

On the other hand, there presumably is a set of actions to make the world more biosecure that can move the needle.

I now have a subjective probability range that the risk of a really bad pandemic event in my lifetime is from very small to small, although I am still trying to figure out a philosophy of putting numbers on this. I don't think I should, lest the numbers be abused. I think I would benefit my readers by coming up with better communication comparables (i.e. me winning the lottery, getting hit by a car as a pedestrian, etc.). But given this range, I still think there is deep aleatoric (future-driven) and epistemic (what is known but I don't know) uncertainty about what the solve is and how things might play out.

The first kind of uncertainty I have is just the baseline level of risk we have to natural pandemics in the first place. This is hard to say. Maybe it would've been easier in the past when the effects of humans on the planet were de minimis. On the natural pandemics side, reading meteorological reports on weather patterns has made me realise the complexities in climate modelling with human effects, and then trying to learn ecology has made me realise the complexities of population dynamics. And so on the whole I think there is deep uncertainty about the frequency of natural pandemics in the future.

And then on the potential of man-made pandemics, that's even more confusing. That level of uncertainty is so deep in every way that I think modelling has to be used with a lot of humility here. One thing I have read about in books about modelling disasters is the use of narrative instead of risk to figure out what's what. And for this I don't regret reading books like *The Dead Hand* or nuclear arms races to get a better handle of what may happen, probabilities be damned.

One thing I do have a little more conviction in is that these types of societal risks are all becoming tied together, like one big soup. A pandemic problem becomes a logistical problem because people have to stay at home, and then that logistical problem exacerbates the odds of a pandemic problem. For example, if we did have a pandemic, then we would have a bunch of highway services likely shut down. And if that was the case, we would then have distribution issues on personal protective equipment to critical workers. Which means that then the pandemic problem would get worse.

I'm reading some books about logistical planning in the 80s and something that impressed me is how interpretable their logistic and inventory models were. On the whole as people vibe code things I would expect this interpretability to get worse, because people get lazy and then trust computers too much, and so if there is an out of sample logistical event, I wonder how equipped we as a species would be to deal with it.

And because this is the case, it seems like we should think about societal resilience as one big puzzle, instead of individual fields. Maybe things should be called 'societal resilience', full stop. Maybe all of the AI safety folks, the logistics folks, the biosecurity folks, the financial resilience folks, should all get together or something. That would be an interesting house party...

And so, conditioning on a bad future, because of logistical uncertainty and unknown unknowns, I feel like biosafety should be attacked with as few assumptions as possible. And for this reason, I'm kinda excited again by looking at the DIY biosecurity experiments I've done. For example, last time I tried to seal my own house, maybe I should revisit that.

Also in my quick foray into scientific instrumentation and testing, I've been wondering how we could make home biosafety as easy to test as a Covid rapid test. I learnt from a building engineer once that you could test if a space was positively pressurised by putting a tea candle. This kind of stuff is really cool! I previously suffered trying to test this with an anemometer. Also, it's no secret that government labs everywhere have been facing budget cuts - and a large amount of those budgets are taken up by the cost of instrumentation; maybe we should have more rigorous discussions over what instrumentation is actually required for the accuracies we need.

Something that I also noticed is that it's really hard to communicate through an elastomeric respirator, versus a cheaper mask. I wonder if there should be like some microphone thing embedded into them for communication? I tried to wear an elastomeric respirator on a three hour drive with my friends from London to Bath, in a car. And I found it pretty hard to communicate, and hot and uncomfortable and sweaty to wear. Also, if they're 3D printed, I wonder if the materials deform and so don't really make a great seal as time goes by.

Anyhow, I'm excited to think about this stuff a bit more and am open to chatting. Please reach out if you wanna talk!

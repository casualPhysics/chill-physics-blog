AI Is Not Going To Kill My Love Of Math

10 September 2026 · On the Navier-Stokes proofs and AI in research.

This week, a company and several individuals have claimed to resolve the Navier-Stokes problem, a Millennium prize problem, through AI assisted proof. For the past year, I think most of my readers are have already been grappling with digesting and adapting to such technological advances. But if you’ve followed the very human-tainted controversy surrounding this recent proof, you’re probably aware that this new set of side effects and existential questions feel even heavier than before.

The Millennium prize problems are something that have inspired me to do math since I was thirteen. It’s why I tried hard to go to Cambridge and learn about analysis and fluid dynamics, and their aesthetic appeal have always enchanted something in my soul. Life has pushed me away from professional academia, but I still am trying to do research math precisely because of those feelings of wonder.

But regrettably, I was surprised at myself when I woke up a few days ago, and the discovery of the counter-example seemed to stir almost no passion nor excitement within me. Rather, it accelerated what my mind has already been thinking about. Questions about AI.

Apart from the economic side of things, like jobs, recent progress has raised questions within me that range from the philosophy of how science is going to move forward, all the way to the social aspects of research. What is math research going to mean? How can I do meaningful work if all the answers are known by an oracle-type computer? Where are the meaningful questions in math going to come from? What is the point of scientific community? Are rich people going to be the only ones able to solve problems with the compute they acquire?

Bear in mind, I’m thinking about this from the viewpoint of a simple math enjoyer, someone who does math in my house for fun. I can’t imagine how the pros are taking things right now.

I am using the word side-effects carefully here, it’s not clear to me that we have problems, nor advances (in the long run, which I’ll explain later) coming from AI assisted proof. It is also not clear if AI assisted proof is net good or net bad for research in the long time limit. In math at least, the realm of theory, success metrics are subjective and its not clear that beating a bunch of benchmarks or winning prizes counts as success in the theory realm. But we do have side effects nonetheless.

As a side note, last year, I thought these side effects would be constrained to mathematicians doing theory, but I now think it is likely that empirical sciences are going to be affected as well. I am both confused but energised to figure out a way forward. The stress of continually having to Bayesian update has made me believe the best strategy is the ‘wake up and adapt to today’ strategy. I just try to work on maximising the expected value of my actions as frequently as I can, for the given moment. I think I have my recent obsession with Age Of Empires II to thank for that one...

Anyways...

Presumably the goal of research is to find fruitful and interesting things. The first set of interesting side effects are a flurry of mixed claims that AI proofs will affect the fruitfulness of research math itself. It is clear that short term wins have been coming fast and hard, like the counterexample of the Jacobian conjecture, the Navier-Stokes resolution, and many other such things. I don’t think anyone can dispute that AI assisted proof has been fruitful in some way. We know more than we did yesterday, we know two more claims are false, and that can’t be a bad thing.

But I, along with most people, am more interested in the long term effects on research, over the next decade or so. And all of these are unclear. Some people seem to think AI is an existential threat and so thinking about the next decade is moot. I don’t know how to feel about that. But as far as math itself goes, some claim that such AI proofs could potentially lead to net detriment in mathematical research in the long run. The mechanics are fuzzy, but here are some claims that are being put out. No one has a crystal ball but these are interesting views.

Terence Tao claims that premature AI assisted proofs may contaminate problems for future inquiry. In this claim Tao suggests that the point of hard problems is the growth of the sub-problems and constructions that come with the attempt to solve it the hard problem. In this paradigm, the result of the problem itself is secondary, the work around it being the main result.

Some historical examples that come to my mind are the whole swathes of modular forms and number theory research that came with the pursuit of solving Fermat’s last theorem. In Tao’s words, if AI prematurely solves something, then contamination means that people will skip this fruitful process of investigation, which was the point in the first place. This investigative act, of asking important questions that might link disparate fields together, finding new questions, or finding the natural perspective for a problem that makes it tractable to solve, is sacred, and skipping it could lead to net detriment to the art of research mathematics as a whole in the long run.

I agree in part with this. In many cases, those novel natural perspectives on a problem are considered research outputs in their own right, and allowing AI proofs would kill that branch of output from research math. Plausible! In a particularly public video on Youtube, Tao used the analogy that AI in proof is like using a helicopter to get to a beautiful waterfall. LLMs might cause mathematicians to miss out on the value of hiking the natural path to that waterfall, missing out the process of more fruitful intermediate research. I am sympathetic to this analogy too, even from a mind- fitness perspective. Imagine if our brains became unfit because we always refused to take the hike, instead always opting for the helicopter.

But, I have mixed feelings about this analogy as a whole. I agree that AI makes it likely that we skip intermediate steps. But objection to this is only really valid if one pursues math for its own sake (which I do). But other people would say that the point of mathematics is to get hard results that we can actually use, and stop at simply just formalising the result, no matter how messy it is. In a world of limited natural resources or state funding, I somewhat understand the view that society can democratically elect to pursue only the results. And I see no issue with going full AI auto-formalisation mode in applied areas of math like cryptography or condensed matter physics. Tangentially, LLMs have shown to string different fields together in unexpected ways. And when I talk to models I also do seem to gain cool new perspectives on things.

Tao’s statements also assume a finiteness about the complexity of the world that I don’t think is true. I take it as an axiom that the universe has an almost infinite level of depth and recursion. And if you think that’s true, then mathematical problems are presumably unbounded in their number and complexity. And so, we should be just racing to solve as many things as possible and make it cleaner later - the problems won’t run out. I don’t think we’ve really given ourself time to probe the areas of math which are impenetrable to AI assisted proof. It might just be the case that we’ve just scratched the surface of everything there is to know, and that Navier Stokes is just the beginning, and none of us have anything to worry about.

However, at the time of writing, I do think that there is a point about AI not being able to find solutions that are beautiful, concise and parsimonious. The examples of proof I have seen so far for research problems appear incredibly terse and dense. They seem to have no respect for what is humanly interpretable. Perhaps the word I’m looking for is that they don’t feel ‘enchanting’, like the work of Dirac, Feynman, Noether and all of the classics.

This is the strongest argument I’ve seen of where AI assisted proofs have a weak point. LLMs so far seem to have no deep concept for what is a beautiful, human interpretable perspective on a problem. This is pure speculation, but maybe its because humans are wired for aesthetics in some weird way that neural networks can’t grok. Maybe there are some deep cultural links that we all have that makes us recognise aesthetics and beauty in a way that avoid the training set of an LLM. But who knows.

On the other hand, I don’t think we understand neural networks enough to rule out the possibility that as they scale, that they just might know what types of interpretations are particularly tasty to humans. In the limit, an LLM might just always be able to give us beautiful and satisfying answers to hard questions. For my sake I do hope we don’t run out of hard questions. But if it does happen, and we don’t have hard questions anymore, then maybe we end up in the equilibrium much like the one competitive chess has reached. We play games, and do maths for leisure, like an insanely cognitively taxing version of paint by numbers. Or, like in competitive chess. Computers can beat chess players without fail. But the games are beautiful precisely because a human has found these strategies. The fact that a human did it is part of the achievement.

I also think that there is a motivational consideration here for the young people in society. If you’re a kid wanting to learn math, that’s presumably a good thing. And if you think that everything out there is to be solved, then you may not want to study math. This is a bad thing. Hell, even if AI hasn’t solved anything, it might push the frontier so far that people don’t have the motivation to get there, and so give up on mathematics as an enterprise outright. I worry about this happening a lot.

To be honest, I don’t know how things are going to shake out on this front. Looking introspectively, I do still love the process of learning math even if its not research level. I spend hours trying to learn things that are presumably well known and don’t really care that I’m spending my time that way. If that means a lifetime of learning how to interpret AI generated solutions for my own understanding, I actually don’t see that as a bad outcome. I also happen to think there are many different ways to look at flowers, and that humans won’t get bored of looking at flowers, and there infinitely many ways to paint and present a flower.

Take a look at the physical sciences. We look at the world around us, we observe reality, and still don’t understand it. Is that so different from trying to comprehend an oracle that rolls out strings of intensely long and complicated logic that solves theoretical problems? I guess it’s quite similar. I consider computers and theory as part of the natural world as well. Feels like a similar game that’s worthwhile.

Anyways, the fuck knows. I’m going to bed and when I wake up things will probably be different in the world. But I do know that I’ll still be chipping a way at that probability example sheet I’ve been working on, I just love the game too much.

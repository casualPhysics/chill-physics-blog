Writing What I See

5 September 2026 · On Da Vinci And Virginia Woolf

I have started to read a book on the history of Renaissance scientific thought by Blake and Ducasse, Theories of Scientific Method: The Renaissance Through the Nineteenth Century (1960). I found this text in a random cinema bookshop in Hay-on-Wye and I have no idea how acclaimed this particular account of history is amongst academic circles. But sometimes, niche texts on scientific history do have some useful gems for simple people like me, one of which I seem to have discovered today.

The first chapter of the book caught my eye. Let me explain. Both the recent technological progress with LLM models, and fragile geopolitical situation in Europe have left me grappling with how to tell what is true. I read something and there is a non-zero probability it is the result of some averaged out mushing of matrix numbers, generated probabilistically or through motivated means of political actors. And so I remain uncertain if this type of averaged response uncovers anything of personal value to me.

And as such, I have been thinking a lot about how to anchor myself closer to reality. But the authors appear to have found a partial answer to my conundrum through the exposition of a conflict between Leonardo Da Vinci and the 15th Century humanists.

The authors illustrate the difference between their ways of studying the world, which seem to be relevant to the level of indirection humans have between them and reality today.

The two different ways are as follows. As a way to obtain knowledge about the world, the 15th Century Humanists preferred to study and defer to the ancients of authority, studying works of Greek mathematics, ethics and classics. If one is unsure of the buoyancy of a shape in fluid, then refer to the works of Archimedes.

But Da Vinci saw a more virtuous pursuit in the direct observation of nature itself. The most virtuous path to studying nature was through experience and through the human senses. As part of the pursuit of understanding, one sees, hears, smells, or measures the natural world, much like Volterra's early discovery of electricity through licking frogs. And once something is experienced, the observer then records that experience in detail. Of course, mathematical rigour was still to be expected, but the root comes from experience and measurement.

Da Vinci also believed this about painting. It was more virtuous to paint what is in front of you, instead of imitating other paintings. The artist should seek to capture reality by observing it directly. The book pushes strongly that Da Vinci believed that knowledge comes from experience. It cites The Literary Works Of Leonardo da Vinci Compiled and Edited From the Original Manuscripts - by Jean Paul Richter. Some attributed quotes to Da Vinci that may or may not have been lost in translation include

  • 'All our knowledge has its origins in our perceptions '*

  • 'Experience never errs; it is only your judgements that err by promising themslves effects such as are not caused by your experiments'*

Da Vinci was also an observational maximalist and believed in striving for detail when observing something. He also thought that experience through the senses could also be enhanced by instrumentation. He thought that drawing was almost necessary for true observation. When I went to Florence in August I really felt compelled to figure out how I could master the art of scientific drawing.

I can't help but feel something similar when reading about Virginia Woolf's essays on fiction. In 'Modern Fiction', Woolf questions the need for comprehendable structure and narrative in the traditional novel. Why do classical forms of literature like the poem, tragedy or comedy have structure when the human mind is anything but structured? As Woolf writes, 'The mind receives a myriad of impressions' in an ordinary day, without respect to temporal order or reason, and so should writing not be true to observation and paints thoughts as the 'incessant shower of atoms' that they are? In this vein she criticises the materialists, and instead pays homage to writers like James Joyce.

From Woolf's essay on modern fiction:

Write it as it is I guess. In my writing and work I am trying to strive for closeness to reality. Da Vinci would tell me to always just do an experiment where I can, and to use my eyes and my apparatus. But I also am trying to make it as observationally accurate as possible to my readers, recording my thoughts in whatever order they fall, like Woolf's shower of atoms. It is what it is. In that spirit, I cycled along the Thames on my Brompton, down till Wandsworth. I can't help thinking about how beautiful London is on a warm and sunny autumn afternoon.

  2. Jean Paul Richter (comp. & ed.), The Literary Works of Leonardo da Vinci, 2 vols. (London: Sampson Low, Marston, Searle & Rivington, 1883).

The Value Of Experience

29 September 2026 · On empiricism and recording observations

How can an independent scientist find the best way to contribute to human knowledge? One might observe scientists in the best academic institutions, and then mimic their behaviour. Academic institutions have done a great job so far at doing research. But there are reasons why some behaviours, even in some of the best institutions, may be inefficient. They may be unrelated to the scientific process entirely. They may also be counterproductive. If you're an independent scientist, finding these inefficiencies and then avoiding them is likely to give you a slight edge against the big shops.

For example, senior academics in Cambridge wear gowns to dinner. Do you need to do that to be a scientist? Obviously not. However, there are other things that blur the lines between doing good science and appearing to do good science. Peer review is likely to be useful in some ways, and genuinely positive for the scientific process. Feedback from others helps in spotting mistakes. But my academic friends find frustrations with other parts of the process. Could peer review simply be the best option out of a set of bad options? It's unclear, and first-principles thinking is required to untangle signal from noise. We think about this at the Independent Science Society.

I've been reading books on the philosophy of science to understand what good scientific reasoning really is. I wrote about my method for reading things in my previous post. I've been following that method for Peter Godfrey-Smith's Theory and Reality (2003) and Karl Popper's The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934, English translation 1959). Admittedly, the style of these philosophy books is hard to digest. They tend to include a lot of propositional logic. I aim to simplify them and give my thoughts here.

First, Godfrey-Smith has convinced me that the word 'science' is ill-defined. Several fields have both entered and left the perception of being scientific. Theoretical physics in the 1950s was unmistakably a science geared towards understanding particle physics. Today it's less clear. Tests of string theory need energies far higher than the Large Hadron Collider can provide. Sabine Hossenfelder, in Lost in Math (2018), has argued that some theoretical physics departments now focus on inspiring new mathematics, seeking aesthetics instead of empirical results. On the other hand, economics and the social sciences seem to have taken on semi-scientific status. They have incorporated mathematics, use weird things called p-values and t-tests, and have put the word 'science' in their names.

So it's not clear what the meaning of science actually is. The word is probably abused. A better question, then, is how one can model the natural world. Godfrey-Smith's book is a review of such methods. The chapters are names of philosophies that I have heard of but have never taken the time to properly understand: empiricism, logical positivism, induction and Kuhnian revolutions.

Empiricism is the philosophy that all knowledge about the world is born of experience. In this framework, knowledge is created downstream of observation. Observation is done through the senses: sight, sound, touch and taste. The senses can be enhanced through instrumentation, like the microscope. In Godfrey-Smith's view, the difference between the scientific method and everyday empiricism is the detail and extensiveness of the practitioner's observations. This separates a scientist from someone who casually notices that you shouldn't touch a boiling cauldron. Da Vinci painstakingly drew what he saw. Darwin wrote down everything he noticed in the Galápagos Islands. Mendel collected a lot of data about peas.

Empiricism as a method is encouraging because there is no qualitative difference between a scientist and a non-scientist. It is merely a matter of degree. Anyone can aspire to the scientific rigour of the legends of past centuries. I aim to look closer at what I am doing, and record my observations in greater detail.

Pure empiricism feels different from knowledge about the world being born of logic. In mathematics, I can claim that 2+2=4 without any prior experience. And sometimes mathematically true things can later be confirmed by experience. The discovery of antimatter was born of Dirac's mathematical arguments. Similarly, the properties of black holes were predicted from Einstein's equations on mathematical grounds long before the LIGO experiments confirmed them. Being trained as a mathematician has biased me in favour of this style of thinking.

That being said, I'm starting to accept that a lot of good can be done from experience. Theories will ultimately turn out wrong in some way. And even if the underlying theory is replaced in the light of new evidence in the future, the observations can still take you far. Caloric theory was used to explain thermodynamics well before Boltzmann. The miasma theory of disease turned out to be wrong, and yet it was still effective at preventing deaths before germ theory was discovered.

The book argues that whilst empiricism may feel obvious as a theory, we have plenty of examples where humans have acted contrary to experience. History has had human sacrifices and witchcraft. Godfrey-Smith references Carl Hempel's account of Ignaz Semmelweis's ousting from Viennese hospitals when he tried to push his empirical study of hand washing preventing deaths from childbed fever. We have ideologies that prevent us from looking at the best evidence. Isaac Asimov's short essays in The Roving Mind (1983) on creationism in education serve as a particularly good example of humans acting against experience.

Sometimes we might act contrary to experience because of flawed statistical reasoning. In The Art of Uncertainty (2024), David Spiegelhalter lists several cases of mothers being tried for the deaths of their children due to expert witnesses and lawyers arguing on the basis of 'coincidence', rather than physical evidence.

In any case, I've increased my prior that the phrase 'see it to believe it' is useful. A purple duck is a purple duck, baby!

Books mentioned

  • Peter Godfrey-Smith, Theory and Reality: An Introduction to the Philosophy of Science (University of Chicago Press, 2003)

  • Karl Popper, The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934; English edition 1959)

  • Sabine Hossenfelder, Lost in Math: How Beauty Leads Physics Astray (Basic Books, 2018)

  • Carl Hempel, Philosophy of Natural Science (Prentice-Hall, 1966), on Semmelweis

  • Isaac Asimov, The Roving Mind (Prometheus Books, 1983)

  • David Spiegelhalter, The Art of Uncertainty (Pelican, 2024)
